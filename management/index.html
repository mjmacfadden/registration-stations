Can You Delegate Without Seeing the Result?

What You Need

Person A — The Delegator

Sees the secret target image on this screen.

Describes it step-by-step using only shapes, directions, and measurements — never the object's name.

Person B — The Artist

Sits back-to-back with Person A, holding blank paper.

Draws only what they hear — never seeing the target image until the reveal.

The Scenario

Great managers must give directions clearly enough that someone else can execute a task correctly — without being able to see the finished product in advance, and often without being in the same room. This exercise simulates exactly that challenge in miniature.

The Golden Rules:

  • Rule 1: Person A may only describe shapes, directions, and measurements (e.g. "Draw a 2-inch square in the bottom-left corner").
  • Rule 2: Person A can never say what the final object actually is (no saying "chimney" or "it's a house").
  • Rule 3: Person B stays completely silent the entire time — no questions, no clarifications, no "like this?" Just listen and draw exactly what you hear.

When time is up, you'll compare the Artist's drawing to the real target image and discuss what made the instructions land — or completely miss.

Curse of Knowledge

Managers who already understand a task forget what it's like not to have the full picture. Instructions that feel obvious to you can be meaningless without shared context.

No Feedback Loop

With zero clarifying questions allowed, small misunderstandings can never be caught or corrected mid-task — they just compound until the final result is revealed.

Precision Matters

Vague instructions like "draw a line in the middle" produce wildly different results depending on how each person interprets "middle" or "line."

Reveal Instructor Key Takeaways & Management Applications

1. Build Feedback Loops: Managers who build in check-ins ("does this make sense so far?") catch misunderstandings before they compound into wasted work.

2. Use Concrete, Measurable Language: Replace vague adjectives ("big," "near the top") with specific shapes, sizes, and positions whenever possible.

3. Match the Channel to the Task: High-ambiguity tasks need two-way channels (calls, in-person); low-ambiguity, routine tasks can often be delegated one-way (email, written SOPs).
